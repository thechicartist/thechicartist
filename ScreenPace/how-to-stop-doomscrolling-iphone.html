How to stop doomscrolling on iPhone

You do not need more willpower. You need a stopping point — and a way to hold it when the feed gets interesting.

Doomscrolling rarely starts as a decision. You open your phone to check one thing, and twenty minutes later you are still scrolling, a little tired, a little annoyed with yourself, and no closer to whatever you actually wanted to do.

November 18, 2026 · 6 minute read

The useful question is not “Why can't I stop?” It is “What would make stopping easier?”

Most advice about doomscrolling treats it as a failure of self-control. Delete the apps. Try harder. Just stop. That advice fails for a simple reason: the feed is designed to be hard to leave. A book has a last page and a television episode has credits. Many feeds remove those natural stopping points — fresh content arrives with each movement, and the next item might be more interesting than the last. That uncertainty makes “just one more” easy to repeat.

So the fix is not to become a different person. It is to change the environment around the scroll, so stopping becomes the easy option instead of the hard one.

Start here

1. Name your purpose before you open the app

Before you open a feed, finish this sentence: “I am here to…” If there is no answer, pause for one breath. Most doomscrolling starts with an automatic opening — you unlock your phone and reach a feed before deciding what you wanted. A one-second pause breaks that automatic loop, and often that is enough.

It also helps to decide how long you will stay, and what you will do when that time ends. “Five minutes, then I will make tea” is a plan. “I will try not to scroll too long” is not.

Make leaving easy

2. Give the scroll an ending

Since the feed will not give you an ending, you have to create one. The most reliable way is a small, repeatable limit — not a promise to avoid the app all day, which most people break by noon, but a short allowance you can actually keep.

That is the idea behind hourly limits. Give a distracting app five minutes an hour, and when the minutes are spent, the app is shielded until the next hour, when a fresh allowance arrives. One unplanned scroll costs you an hour, not the whole day — and every hour is a fresh start. Our guide to hourly vs daily screen time limits explains why this works better than a single daily limit for most people.

Change the setup

3. Make the phone slightly harder to reach

Small friction beats big resolutions. Move the most distracting apps off your home screen, so opening them takes a deliberate search instead of a tap. Turn off badges and banners for feeds, so the phone stops calling you back. Charge your phone outside the bedroom at night, so the first and last hour of the day belong to you instead of the feed.

None of these steps is dramatic. Together, they add up: each one makes the automatic open a little less automatic.

Replace, don't just remove

4. Give your hands somewhere else to go

Empty time naturally pulls us back to a screen. If you remove scrolling without replacing it, the boredom that drove the scrolling is still there, and the phone is still the easiest answer. Keep something easier to reach than the phone: a book, walking shoes, a sketchbook, a half-finished project. A small creative task gives that time somewhere better to land.

This is also why doomscrolling gets worse late at night. You are tired, your willpower is spent, and the phone is right there. A bedtime routine that does not involve a screen — even a short one — protects the hour when you are least able to resist.

When you slip

5. Review the pattern, not the failure

You will slip. Everyone does. The question is what you do next. Instead of deciding the day is ruined and scrolling for another hour, ask what was happening before the unplanned scroll: boredom, stress, fatigue, or simple habit? Then adjust the environment — move the app, change the limit, change the routine — rather than insulting yourself.
