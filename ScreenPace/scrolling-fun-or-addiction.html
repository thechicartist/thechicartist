Scrolling: fun or addiction?

How to stay in control—without giving up the fun

A feed can entertain you, connect you, or teach you something new. Trouble starts when a quick check repeatedly becomes time you did not mean to give away.

ScreenPace Journal
September 4, 2026 · 6 minute read

The useful question is not “Am I addicted?” It is “Can I stop when I intended to?”

There is no honest universal number that turns ordinary screen time into a problem. Watching a tutorial, messaging a friend, and drifting through short videos for an hour may all register as “screen time,” but they do not play the same role in your life.

Scrolling is probably still serving you when you choose it, enjoy it, and leave without much struggle. It deserves attention when it regularly takes priority over sleep, movement, work, relationships, or the things you wanted to make and do.

Notice the pattern

Fun has an ending. A loop keeps asking for one more.

One long evening does not define your relationship with your phone. Look for repeated patterns instead:

Automatic opening

You unlock your phone and reach a feed before deciding what you wanted.

Time disappears

A planned five-minute check often becomes much longer.

Stopping feels unusually hard

You notice you are done, yet keep moving to the next post.

Something important moves aside

Sleep, meals, movement, focused work, or conversation repeatedly waits.

These are prompts for curiosity, not a diagnosis. The goal is to notice whether your behavior still matches your intentions.

Designed without a stopping cue

If leaving feels difficult, it is not simply a character flaw.

A book has a last page and a television episode has credits. Many feeds remove those natural stopping points. Fresh content arrives with each movement, and the next item might be more interesting than the last. That uncertainty makes “just one more” easy to repeat.

The American Psychological Association specifically identifies infinite scroll and missing time limits as features that can make disengaging harder for young people. The same design lesson is useful for adults: when a product removes the ending, creating your own ending can help.

A gentle reset

Make the next scroll a choice again.

  1. Name the purpose

    Before opening the app, finish this sentence: “I am here to…” If there is no answer, pause for one breath.

  2. Choose a small stopping point

    Try one short allowance instead of promising to avoid the app all day. A boundary you can repeat is more useful than an extreme rule you resent.

  3. Let a tool remember for you

    Willpower gets tired. A visible timer or an hourly limit can hold the decision you made before the feed became interesting.

  4. Give your hands somewhere else to go

    Keep a pencil, brush, book, walking shoes, or half-finished project easier to reach than the phone.

  5. Review without punishment

    Ask what was happening before an unplanned scroll—boredom, stress, fatigue, or simple habit—and adjust the environment rather than insulting yourself.

A 30-second check

Before you open the feed…

  • What am I opening it for?
  • How long do I want to stay?
  • What will I do when that time ends?

If you can answer those three questions, the phone is much more likely to remain a tool instead of becoming the plan.
